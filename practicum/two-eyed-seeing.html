Two-Eyed Seeing

The phrase comes from Mi'kmaw Elder Albert Marshall, who describes Etuaptmumk — Two-Eyed Seeing — as learning to see with one eye the strengths of Indigenous knowledge and ways of knowing, and with the other the strengths of Western knowledge, and learning to use both eyes together.

It is his idea and his community's, not mine. I use the phrase because it names something I could not otherwise describe, and I try to use it the way it was given.

Why it matters in this field

Psychedelic medicine sits on ground that was never empty. Many of the substances now moving through Western clinical trials have been used ceremonially for generations by people who developed sophisticated frameworks for preparation, dosing, setting, and integration long before those words entered a research protocol.

Western pharmacology can say what a molecule does to a receptor. It has been much slower to say what a community does to a person in distress, or what ritual does to meaning, or why the same dose produces different outcomes in different rooms.

Neither eye sees the whole thing.

What it looks like in practice

Holding two accounts at once without collapsing one into the other. Not "the ceremony is really just placebo," and not "the pharmacology is beside the point." Both descriptions are real, and they answer different questions.

Not extracting. The strong temptation in this field is to take the practice and leave the people — to isolate a compound, borrow a ritual structure, and cite tradition as marketing while the communities involved see nothing back. That is the failure mode Two-Eyed Seeing is meant to prevent, and naming it here is not a guarantee that I always get it right.

Knowing whose knowledge it is. I am a settler pharmacist. I have worked in northern and Indigenous communities and I have learned from people there, but I do not hold that knowledge, cannot speak for it, and would not teach it.

Where I bring it

The pharmacological eye is the one I was trained in, and it is what I offer a clinical team: interactions, contraindications, screening, the specifics of what a substance does in a body. The other eye is one I am still learning to open, in a graduate programme that takes spirituality seriously as part of care rather than as decoration around it.

A note of caution

Two-Eyed Seeing is not a licence to blend everything. Elder Marshall's framing is about weaving, not merging. It requires each way of knowing to stay itself, intact and separately valid. Careless syncretism — a bit of ceremony, a bit of clinic, no accountability to either — is the opposite of what he described.

Education, not medical advice.
